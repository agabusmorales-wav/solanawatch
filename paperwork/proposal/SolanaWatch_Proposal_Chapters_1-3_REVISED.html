Chapter 1: Introduction

Project Context

Solanaceae crops, which include tomatoes, peppers, eggplants, and potatoes, are among the economically important vegetable crops cultivated in the Philippines. According to the Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA, 2026), tomatoes, eggplants, and potatoes are recognized as major vegetable crops that significantly impact the country's vegetable production and the livelihoods of many Filipino farmers. According to the latest PSA Major Vegetables and Rootcrops Quarterly Bulletin, eggplant remains one of the most heavily produced crops in this category, with production volumes consistently exceeding 100,000 metric tons per quarter, while tomato production regularly reaches between 70,000 to 93,000 metric tons depending on the harvest season. Their high market demand and nutritional value make them important to food security and the agricultural economy. Given their economic importance, effective disease management is essential to sustaining the productivity of Solanaceae crops and reducing crop losses among farmers (Aquino et al., 2024).

Among the diseases that significantly affect Solanaceae crops are late blight and bacterial wilt, both of which can cause severe production losses when preventive measures are not implemented promptly (Aumentado & Balendres, 2024; Subedi et al., 2024). The development of these diseases is closely associated with environmental conditions within the field. Late Blight (Phytophthora infestans) thrives in environments with high relative humidity, prolonged leaf wetness, and moderate temperatures, whereas Bacterial Wilt (Ralstonia pseudosolanacearum) is more prevalent under warm soil temperatures and high soil moisture. Because these environmental conditions may change rapidly throughout the day, periods favorable for disease development can occur before visible symptoms appear on the crops. Consequently, delayed intervention may result in harvest losses ranging from 50% to 90%, significantly affecting farmers' productivity and income.

Based on recent agricultural literature and field assessments of local cultivation practices, disease monitoring in soil-grown Solanaceae crops is still primarily performed through manual visual inspection. Existing research indicates that farmers typically identify diseases only after observing visible symptoms such as leaf spots, discoloration, wilting, or general plant decline. Although this practice is widely adopted across small-scale and smallholder farms due to its simplicity and zero initial cost, plant pathology studies emphasize that visual scouting often detects diseases only after the infection has heavily progressed. Consequently, growers have a limited opportunity to implement timely preventive measures, reducing the overall effectiveness of localized disease management practices and increasing their eventual dependence on chemical treatments to control disease spread. Given that microclimatic conditions fluctuate rapidly throughout the day, relying solely on visual inspection is technically insufficient to identify disease-conducive conditions before irreversible crop damage occurs.

Continuous environmental monitoring could provide farmers with earlier indications of disease risk by tracking environmental conditions that favor pathogen development. However, implementing such monitoring remains challenging, particularly in small-scale farming environments. Many farming sites experience unstable internet connectivity and occasional power interruptions, which disrupt IoT-based monitoring systems that rely on continuous network access and external power sources. These interruptions may prevent the continuous collection and transmission of environmental data, resulting in incomplete environmental records and reducing the reliability of disease-risk assessment during periods when timely information is most needed.

Recent advancements in smart agriculture have introduced technologies that improve environmental monitoring and farm management. The integration of the Internet of Things (IoT) has enabled automated collection of environmental data through distributed sensors, allowing farmers to remotely monitor field conditions and improve decision-making (Mastul et al., 2023; Ndjuluwa et al., 2023). Existing agricultural technologies include IoT-based automated irrigation systems (Idris et al., 2024), commercial smart farming platforms utilizing cloud-based analytics, regional weather-based disease forecasting systems, and image-based Artificial Intelligence (AI) applications for plant disease detection (Jafar et al., 2024). While these technologies have contributed to modern agricultural practices, they also present several limitations. Image-based AI systems generally identify diseases only after visible symptoms have developed, regional weather forecasting platforms may not accurately represent the microclimatic conditions within individual farms, and many commercial IoT solutions require substantial financial investment, technical expertise, reliable internet connectivity, and uninterrupted power to operate effectively.

Despite these advancements, no single solution adequately combines localized environmental monitoring, resilient data collection during temporary internet and power interruptions, and environmental-based disease-risk assessment into an affordable platform suitable for small-scale Solanaceae crop production. There is also a need for a system capable of evaluating disease risk using environmental conditions measured directly in the field, rather than relying solely on visible symptoms or regional weather information. Such a system would enable farmers to recognize disease-conducive conditions earlier and implement preventive measures before significant crop damage occurs.

To address these challenges, the proponents will develop SolanaWatch, an Internet of Things (IoT)-based disease early warning system designed for soil-grown Solanaceae crops. The proposed system will continuously monitor localized environmental parameters associated with the development of late blight and bacterial wilt. Environmental data will be collected continuously and will be synchronized with a cloud-based backend for storage and analysis using established disease-risk models and environmental threshold values reported in plant pathology literature. During temporary internet interruptions, environmental data will be stored locally and will be automatically synchronized with the cloud database once connectivity is restored. The processed information will be presented through a web-based dashboard that will enable users to monitor field conditions, review historical environmental records, and receive timely notifications when environmental conditions become favorable for disease development.

Purpose and Description

The purpose of this study is to develop SolanaWatch, an Internet of Things (IoT)-based disease early warning system for soil-grown Solanaceae crops. The proposed system aims to assist farmers in monitoring environmental conditions associated with the development of late blight and bacterial wilt by automatically collecting localized environmental data, evaluating disease risk through a rule-based assessment using environmental threshold values reported in plant pathology literature, and providing timely notifications when environmental conditions become favorable for disease development. By supporting the early identification of disease-conducive conditions, the proposed system is designed to enhance farmers' decision-making in implementing appropriate preventive disease management practices, as well as contribute to efforts to minimize crop losses and unnecessary pesticide applications.

Intended Beneficiaries

The proposed system is expected to directly benefit several agricultural stakeholder groups: - Small-Scale and Smallholder Solanaceae Farmers: Provides an affordable, accessible, and automated tool to monitor crop microclimates in real time, receive early visual warnings, and implement timely preventive actions before visible symptoms or severe crop damage occur. - Agricultural Technicians and Extension Personnel: Equips field officers and technicians from local government units (LGUs) and agricultural agencies with centralized historical environmental records to support localized advisory services and proactive farm management. - Agricultural Researchers: Provides empirical field data on localized microclimatic trends, soil hydrothermal dynamics, and disease favorability conditions to support future studies on Solanaceae crop health in the Philippines.

Overview of the Proposed System

The proposed SolanaWatch system consists of three main conceptual subsystems: 1. Environmental Sensing and Edge Node Subsystem: Deployed directly in the crop field, this subsystem automatically and periodically measures localized canopy and soil parameters, including air temperature, relative humidity, leaf wetness, soil temperature, and soil moisture. It incorporates local offline data storage to preserve records during temporary connectivity interruptions, supported by a dedicated solar-assisted power subsystem for continuous operation. 2. Cloud Data Management and Disease-Risk Assessment Subsystem: Receives transmitted telemetry, securely stores historical records in a cloud database, manages automatic synchronization of offline data, and evaluates multi-parameter readings against established plant pathology threshold models. 3. Web-Based Monitoring Interface: Presents real-time field measurements, historical visualization charts, device status indicators, and color-coded early warning notifications to authenticated users through a standard web browser.

Scope and Delimitations

Scope

This project covers the design, development, and evaluation of SolanaWatch, an Internet of Things (IoT)-based disease early warning system for soil-grown Solanaceae crops. Specifically, the project includes the following:

  • Target Crops: The study focuses on soil-grown Solanaceae crops cultivated in the Philippines, specifically tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), eggplant (Solanum melongena), pepper (Capsicum annuum), and potato (Solanum tuberosum).
  • Target Diseases: Disease-risk modeling focuses on two major pathologies: Late Blight (Phytophthora infestans) and Bacterial Wilt (Ralstonia pseudosolanacearum).
  • Environmental Sensing: Real-time acquisition of localized parameters, including ambient air temperature, relative humidity, root-zone soil temperature (at 10 cm depth), soil moisture (at 10 cm depth), and foliar leaf wetness duration.
  • Edge Deployment: The system utilizes a single fixed monitoring node installed at a representative location within the selected cultivation plot to capture microclimatic conditions surrounding the crop canopy.
  • Power Resilience: Integration of a solar-assisted power subsystem and rechargeable battery to support continuous 24/7 outdoor operation.
  • Offline Data Logging & Sync: Local storage on an onboard memory card during network interruptions, with automatic batch synchronization once internet connectivity is restored.
  • Rule-Based Disease-Risk Assessment: Evaluation of collected sensor readings against literature-supported environmental threshold models to determine current disease risk levels (Low, Moderate, High).
  • Cloud Backend & Database: Centralized storage and management of historical environmental records.
  • Web-Based Dashboard: Real-time monitoring displays, historical trend visualizations, device status tracking, and early warning notifications.
  • User Authentication: Role-based access control to secure system data and administrative functions.
  • System Evaluation: Technical verification of sensor accuracy, communication range, power autonomy, and software quality evaluation based on ISO/IEC 25010 (functional suitability, performance efficiency, reliability, usability via the System Usability Scale).

Delimitations

The project is delimited to the following:

  • Environmental Risk Assessment vs. Direct Biological Diagnosis: The proposed system is designed to evaluate microclimatic favorability (disease risk) based on physical environmental thresholds. It does not perform biological pathogen detection, DNA/PCR analysis, optical leaf spectroscopy, or image-based visual disease diagnosis. A high-risk notification indicates that physical field conditions are conducive to pathogen development, not a confirmed laboratory diagnosis of pathogen presence.
  • Pathologies Covered: The disease-risk assessment is strictly delimited to Late Blight and Bacterial Wilt. Other crop diseases affecting Solanaceae crops (e.g., viral infections, fungal anthracnose, root-knot nematodes) are excluded from the risk engine.
  • Cultivation Method: The system is intended only for soil-grown crops under open-field or screenhouse conditions. Hydroponic, aquaponic, and soilless cultivation media are beyond the scope of this study.
  • Monitoring Node Deployment: The prototype consists of a single stationary sensing node. Mobile rovers, automated ground vehicles, drone-based aerial sensing, and multi-node mesh networks are not included.
  • Wireless Communication: Wireless transmission is delimited to local Wi-Fi connectivity between the node and local gateway/access point. Cellular GSM/4G/5G modems and satellite transceivers are excluded.
  • Actuation and Remediation: The system functions strictly as an early warning and decision-support tool. It does not include automated actuation mechanisms, such as automated pesticide sprayers or motorized irrigation valves.
  • Long-Term Agronomic Studies: System evaluation is conducted during scheduled field testing cycles to validate system reliability and usability. Long-term multi-season studies on crop yield gains, longitudinal economic returns, or aggregate pesticide reduction rates across multi-year cycles are beyond the scope of this capstone project.

Chapter 2: Review of Related Literature, Studies, and Systems

Agriculture continues to play an important role in food production and livelihood, but crop production remains affected by environmental changes and plant diseases that can reduce both yield and quality. Because of this, recent studies have increasingly explored the use of digital technologies and Internet of Things (IoT) systems to assist farmers in monitoring crop conditions (Ndjuluwa et al., 2023; Yesankar et al., 2024). These systems allow environmental information to be collected continuously through sensors and transmitted to users through wireless networks or cloud-based platforms. Instead of relying entirely on manual observation, IoT-based agricultural systems can provide farmers with more frequent information about the conditions surrounding their crops, which may support earlier and more informed management decisions (Suryadevara et al., 2022).

This approach is particularly relevant to Solanaceae crops such as tomatoes, eggplants, peppers, and potatoes because these crops are affected by several diseases whose development is influenced by environmental conditions (Aquino et al., 2024; Aubriot & Knapp, 2022). Among these diseases are Late Blight and Bacterial Wilt, which can cause significant damage to crops when conditions become favorable for their development (Aumentado & Balendres, 2024; Subedi et al., 2024). Previous studies have therefore investigated not only the pathogens themselves but also the environmental conditions associated with disease occurrence. This provides a basis for developing monitoring systems that observe environmental conditions as an early indication of possible disease risk rather than waiting until visible symptoms have already developed.

2.1 Environmental Determinants of Solanaceae Diseases

Late Blight, commonly associated with Phytophthora infestans, is one of the most destructive diseases affecting tomato and potato production worldwide (Mazumdar et al., 2021; University of Florida IFAS, n.d.). Research on the disease has shown that its development is strongly associated with environmental conditions involving temperature, relative humidity, and moisture on plant surfaces. The pathogen develops and sporulates rapidly under moderate temperatures (12°C to 22°C) combined with high relative humidity (≥ 90%). Furthermore, leaf wetness duration (LWD) is recognized as a crucial factor, as free water on leaf surfaces is required for sporangia to germinate and infect plant tissues (Boulton et al., 2023). Established disease forecasting models, such as Wallin’s Severity Values (SV) and BLITECAST, demonstrate that prolonged leaf wetness (≥ 10 continuous hours) within permissive temperature ranges significantly increases infection risk (Boulton et al., 2023; Tao et al., 2024). Because environmental conditions can become favorable before visible lesions appear, monitoring variables such as temperature, humidity, and leaf wetness provides valuable data for early risk warning.

Bacterial Wilt presents a different but related concern because it is associated with soilborne pathogens that affect the vascular system of plants. The disease is caused by Ralstonia pseudosolanacearum (and related strains within the Ralstonia solanacearum species complex), which affects tomatoes, eggplants, and peppers in tropical and subtropical regions (Silva et al., 2024; Subedi et al., 2024). Studies concerning bacterial wilt have examined the relationship between disease occurrence and soil conditions. Jiang et al. (2021), for example, investigated bacterial wilt in tomato fields using soil samples from different locations and found that soil moisture was an important predictor of bacterial wilt occurrence. Their study further demonstrated through greenhouse experiments that excessive soil moisture (≥ 75% field capacity or saturated soil) facilitates bacterial motility and root invasion. Concurrently, warm soil temperatures between 28°C and 35°C accelerate bacterial multiplication within the host vascular bundles, leading to rapid wilting (Silva et al., 2024). These findings provide a strong research-based reason for monitoring both soil temperature and soil moisture rather than treating them only as general agricultural parameters.

The findings from these disease-related studies also show why using only one environmental parameter is insufficient for an agricultural early warning system. Temperature, relative humidity, leaf wetness, soil temperature, and soil moisture describe different aspects of the environment surrounding the crop. A field may have high atmospheric humidity without necessarily having the same amount of moisture on the leaf surface, while soil conditions may differ from conditions measured above the crop. Monitoring several parameters therefore provides a more complete representation of the local conditions in which crops are growing. For this reason, SolanaWatch combines multiple canopy and root-zone environmental measurements rather than relying on a single sensor to determine disease risk.

In recent smart agriculture research, ESP32-based systems have been widely used to collect temperature, humidity, soil moisture, and other environmental information and transmit the measurements to remote platforms (Mohd Khalid et al., 2024). Other computing platforms can also be considered for agricultural monitoring, making the selection of the main controller an important technical consideration. 2.3 Environmental Sensors and Calibration

The sensors used in SolanaWatch were selected based on the environmental variables identified in the literature: - Ambient Air Temperature and Relative Humidity: The DHT22 (AM2302) provides high-accuracy temperature and humidity measurements to characterize foliar conditions (Suryadevara et al., 2022). To prevent direct sunlight from skewing readings, the sensor is housed inside a louvered Stevenson Radiation Shield, ensuring representative ambient airflow while shielding the sensor from solar radiation and rain (Botero-Valencia et al., 2022). - Soil Temperature: The waterproof Dallas DS18B20 digital probe measures root-zone soil temperature via a 1-Wire communication interface, offering high precision (±0.5°C) and corrosion resistance for subsurface soil placement (Faqir et al., 2024). - Soil Moisture: The Capacitive Soil Moisture Sensor v1.2 measures soil volumetric water content using dielectric capacitance. Unlike resistive probes that suffer from electrochemical corrosion and rapid electrode degradation, the capacitive sensor's insulated traces provide long-term field stability (Cabia et al., 2025; Songara & Patel, 2022). - Leaf Surface Wetness: An industrial dielectric leaf wetness sensor with an IP68 waterproof rating measures surface moisture on crop leaves. Interfaced through a MAX485 transceiver using the RS485 Modbus-RTU protocol, it provides differential signal immunity against electrical noise and supports reliable communication over field wiring (Faqir et al., 2024; Tao et al., 2024).

Literature emphasizes that precision agriculture systems require standardized sensor calibration before deployment to eliminate baseline drift and systematic error (Cabia et al., 2025; Mane et al., 2024; Montzka et al., 2020). For capacitive soil moisture sensors, a two-point calibration method (measuring dry-air baseline ADC vs. fully saturated water ADC) establishes an accurate linear conversion for volumetric moisture (Songara & Patel, 2022). Similarly, temperature and humidity sensors are verified against ice-bath references and saturated salt solution chambers (NaCl at 75.3% RH) to maintain high data accuracy (Montzka et al., 2020).

2.4 Solar-Assisted Power and Battery Systems

Continuous monitoring in agricultural fields requires an autonomous, reliable power source because external electrical outlets are unavailable in remote crop areas (Botero-Valencia et al., 2022; Gao et al., 2023).

The original proposal configuration considered a single 3.7V 2600 mAh Lithium-ion battery. However, technical reassessment and power audit calculations revealed that a 3.7V 2600 mAh cell provides only 9.62 Watt-hours (Wh) of nominal energy. When powering the ESP32 (Wi-Fi TX current peaks of 180–240 mA), voltage converters for 12V RS485 sensors, and multiple sensing modules, a 2600 mAh battery depletes in under 16 to 24 hours without continuous direct sunlight. Furthermore, standard Li-ion chemistry has a limited lifespan (300–500 cycles) and is vulnerable to capacity loss under high tropical temperatures (Gao et al., 2023).

To achieve true 24/7 continuous operation, the revised design integrates a solar-assisted power subsystem utilizing Lithium Iron Phosphate (LiFePO₄) chemistry: - LiFePO₄ Battery Storage: Delivers a stable 12.8V rail with high thermal stability (up to 60°C) and an extended cycle life of 2,000 to 4,000 charge cycles at 80% Depth of Discharge (DoD). - Solar PV Harvesting: A 20W Monocrystalline Solar Panel paired with an IP67 MPPT Solar Charge Controller collects solar energy during daylight hours to power the node and replenish the battery. Under average Philippine solar conditions (4.5 Peak Sun Hours per day), the 20W panel generates approximately 67.5 Wh/day, easily replenishing the node's estimated daily requirement of ~2.3 Wh/day within minutes of direct sunlight. - Battery Autonomy: Sizing the battery bank at 12.8V 12Ah (153.6 Wh) guarantees over 20+ days of continuous operation even during extended overcast, rainy, or monsoon periods without sunlight.

2.5 Offline Data Buffering, Wireless Range, and Cloud Synchronization

Continuous operation also requires the system to maintain data integrity when internet connectivity is temporarily unavailable (Rachakonda & Stasiewicz, 2024; Tao et al., 2024). Agricultural fields often experience intermittent Wi-Fi coverage due to distance, weather, or signal interference.

For this reason, SolanaWatch incorporates local edge storage using an onboard SPI MicroSD card module. When the ESP32 cannot establish a network connection, sensor readings are appended sequentially to a local FIFO queue on the memory card. Once Wi-Fi connectivity is restored, the ESP32 executes an idempotent batch synchronization routine with the cloud backend (Tao et al., 2024). Each record is identified by a unique client timestamp and UUID, and the cloud database enforces a composite unique constraint (node_id + recorded_at), preventing duplicate records and ensuring zero data loss during network disruptions.

A clear distinction identified in the literature is the difference between sensor microclimatic coverage and wireless communication range: - Sensor Coverage Area: The localized physical zone (typically 5m to 15m radius in uniform canopy conditions) where point-source sensors accurately reflect the microclimatic and soil environment. - Wireless Communication Range: The radio-frequency transmission distance between the ESP32 and the local Wi-Fi router (typically 30m to 50m Line-of-Sight outdoors), which can be evaluated empirically through RSSI signal strength and packet loss testing (Suryadevara et al., 2022).

2.6 Hardware Prototyping and Outdoor Enclosure Protection

The reliability of an outdoor agricultural device depends heavily on circuit construction and physical packaging (Botero-Valencia et al., 2022; Faqir et al., 2024). While solderless breadboards are commonly used in early lab testing, they are unsuited for agricultural deployment due to high contact resistance (0.1 to 0.5 Ω), loose jumper wires, and rapid oxidation under tropical humidity (> 85% RH).

To ensure long-term field reliability, the final SolanaWatch prototype transitions from breadboard wiring to a custom fabricated Printed Circuit Board (PCB) / proto-shield. Soldered connections provide stable ground referencing, eliminate vibration-induced disconnects, and protect analog signal integrity (< 0.01 Ω contact resistance). The electronic components are housed in an IP65/IP66 weatherproof polycarbonate enclosure with rubber silicone gaskets, waterproof PG7/PG9 cable glands, and internal desiccant packs to prevent moisture condensation.

3.3.3 Power Subsystem Sizing and Mathematical Verification

To guarantee continuous 24/7 autonomous field operation without grid electricity, the daily power consumption, battery capacity, and solar panel ratings were mathematically sized based on measured and datasheet parameters.

1. Daily Energy Consumption Budget (E_daily)

The edge node operates on a 15-minute polling interval (4 cycles per hour = 96 cycles per day): - Active Cycle Duration (t_act): 10 seconds (Sensor warm-up, Modbus query, SD write, Wi-Fi association, HTTP transmission). - Deep Sleep Duration (t_sleep): 890 seconds (ULP timer active, RF and peripherals powered down).

Active Power Consumption (P_act):
P_act = (3.3V × 0.251A) + (12.0V × 0.030A) = 0.828W + 0.360W = 1.188 Watts

Sleep Power Consumption (P_sleep):
P_sleep = (3.3V × 0.0004A) + (12.0V × 0.005A) = 0.00132W + 0.060W = 0.06132 Watts

Energy per Cycle (E_cycle):
E_cycle = [ 1.188 Watts × (10 s / 3600 s) ] + [ 0.06132 Watts × (890 s / 3600 s) ]
E_cycle = 0.00330 Wh + 0.01515 Wh = 0.01845 Watt-hours per cycle

Total Daily Baseline Consumption:
E_daily_base = 96 cycles × 0.01845 Wh = 1.7712 Watt-hours per day

Applying a 30% engineering safety margin (× 1.30) to account for Wi-Fi retries, boot sequences, and temperature variations:
E_daily_design = 1.7712 Wh/day × 1.30 ≈ 2.30 Watt-hours per day

2. Battery Capacity Sizing (C_battery)

To provide a minimum of 3 Days of Complete Zero-Sunlight Autonomy (N_days = 3) at 80% Depth of Discharge (DoD = 0.80) and 88% DC buck converter efficiency (η_reg = 0.88):

C_Wh = (E_daily_design × N_days) / (DoD × η_reg)
C_Wh = (2.30 Wh × 3) / (0.80 × 0.88) = 6.90 / 0.704 ≈ 9.80 Watt-hours

For a 12.8V LiFePO₄ battery chemistry:
C_Ah_required = 9.80 Wh / 12.8V ≈ 0.766 Ampere-hours (766 mAh)

Engineering Selection Justification:
The selected 12.8V 12Ah LiFePO₄ battery pack delivers 153.6 Watt-hours of total storage. The actual battery autonomy is calculated as:
Actual Autonomy = (153.6 Wh × 0.80 × 0.88) / 2.30 Wh/day ≈ 47.0 Days of Continuous Autonomy
This guarantees that the node will maintain 100% operational uptime through multi-week Philippine monsoon periods.

3. Solar PV Panel Sizing (P_solar)

Based on the Philippine national average solar insolation of 4.5 Peak Sun Hours per day (PSH = 4.5 h) and an overall system efficiency of 75% (η_sys = 0.75):

P_solar_required = E_daily_design / (PSH × η_sys)
P_solar_required = 2.30 Wh / (4.5 h × 0.75) = 2.30 / 3.375 ≈ 0.68 Watts

Engineering Selection Justification:
The selected 20W Monocrystalline Solar Panel generates approximately:
Daily Energy Generation = 20W × 4.5 h × 0.75 = 67.5 Watt-hours per day
This exceeds the daily requirement by a factor of 29, allowing the battery to fully replenish its consumed daily power within 15 minutes of direct sunlight or sustain positive charging under diffuse cloudy daylight.

1. Late Blight (Phytophthora infestans) Severity Algorithm

The Late Blight algorithm computes an Adapted Wallin Severity Value (SV) tracking cumulative leaf wetness duration:

  • Condition 1 (Low Risk - Green): Leaf Wetness Duration (LWD) < 6 continuous hours, regardless of temperature. (SV = 0)
  • Condition 2 (Moderate Risk - Yellow):
  • Sub-case A: 6 ≤ LWD < 10 continuous hours with Air Temperature between 12°C and 22°C. (SV = 1)
  • Sub-case B: 10 ≤ LWD < 15 continuous hours with Air Temperature between 12°C and 22°C. (SV = 2)
  • Condition 3 (High Risk - Red Alert): LWD ≥ 15 continuous hours with Air Temperature between 12°C and 22°C, or LWD ≥ 10 continuous hours with Relative Humidity ≥ 90%. (SV = 3; triggers immediate early warning alert).

2. Bacterial Wilt (Ralstonia pseudosolanacearum) Hydrothermal Score

The Bacterial Wilt index computes a weighted soil hydrothermal risk score (R_BW):

R_BW = (0.50 × S_ST) + (0.50 × S_SM)

Where: - Soil Temperature Factor (S_ST): - S_ST = 1.0, if Soil Temperature is between 28°C and 35°C (Optimal bacterial proliferation). - S_ST = 0.6, if Soil Temperature is between 25°C and 28°C. - S_ST = 0.2, if Soil Temperature is between 20°C and 25°C. - S_ST = 0.0, if Soil Temperature is below 20°C or above 38°C. - Soil Moisture Factor (S_SM): - S_SM = 1.0, if Soil Moisture ≥ 75% VWC (Saturated / Waterlogged conditions). - S_SM = 0.6, if Soil Moisture is between 60% and 75% VWC. - S_SM = 0.2, if Soil Moisture is between 40% and 60% VWC (Optimal crop field capacity). - S_SM = 0.0, if Soil Moisture is below 40% VWC (Dry soil).

Bacterial Wilt Risk Level Classification: - LOW RISK (Green): R_BW < 0.40 - MODERATE RISK (Yellow): 0.40 ≤ R_BW < 0.70 - HIGH RISK (Red Alert): R_BW ≥ 0.70 (triggers immediate early warning notification).

3.5 Implementation Plan

This section presents the implementation plan for the proposed SolanaWatch system. It identifies the internal and external environments in which the system will operate, discusses feasibility studies, and outlines deployment procedures.

3.5.1 Implementation Environments

Internal Environment

The internal environment consists of the hardware, software, researchers, and users directly involved in the operation and maintenance of the SolanaWatch system. These components work together to collect environmental data, analyze disease risk, and deliver timely notifications through the web-based monitoring platform.

The implementation of SolanaWatch is expected to improve the efficiency of disease monitoring by automating environmental data collection and analysis. Instead of relying solely on manual observation, users can continuously monitor field conditions through the web dashboard. The system also reduces the risk of human error in environmental monitoring while providing centralized storage of historical sensor data for future reference.

Within the development team, the implementation process encourages collaboration among hardware integration, embedded programming, backend development, and user interface design. Because the project follows the Prototyping Model, improvements identified during testing can be incorporated before the system's final deployment.

External Environment

The external environment refers to the agricultural setting where the SolanaWatch system will be deployed, including smallholder and small-scale farmers, agricultural extension technicians, environmental conditions, internet connectivity, and other external factors that may influence system performance.

The proposed system is expected to positively affect smallholder Solanaceae farmers by providing early warnings whenever environmental conditions become favorable for the development of late blight and bacterial wilt. Through timely notifications, farmers can perform preventive actions before visible disease symptoms appear, reducing potential crop losses and improving crop management practices.

Environmental factors such as rainfall, extreme temperatures, prolonged humidity, unstable internet connectivity, and power interruptions may influence the operation of the system. To minimize these effects, SolanaWatch incorporates local MicroSD edge logging, an autonomous solar-assisted LiFePO₄ battery subsystem, and automatic cloud synchronization once connectivity is restored. These features improve system reliability during field deployment.

3.5.2 Feasibility Studies

Technical Feasibility

The technical viability of SolanaWatch is supported by the selection of the ESP32 platform, which provides sufficient processing power, rich peripheral buses, and integrated wireless connectivity for real-time environmental monitoring. The software stack, utilizing Node.js for backend logic and Turso as a distributed cloud database, ensures low-latency data synchronization and high availability. Furthermore, the implementation of a local logging fail-safe via the MicroSD card module and a custom PCB shield provides a robust solution for preserving data integrity and electrical reliability in agricultural field environments.

Operational Feasibility

The proposed system is operationally feasible because it is designed to complement existing farming practices without requiring significant changes to daily routines. Once installed in the crop field, the IoT monitoring device continuously gathers environmental data and automatically uploads the collected information to the cloud.

Users interact with the system primarily through the web-based dashboard, where they can monitor environmental conditions, review historical records, and receive disease-risk notifications. Since the dashboard is accessible using standard web browsers on mobile phones or computers, no specialized software installation is required. The incorporation of automated data collection, cloud synchronization, and intuitive visualization allows farmers and technicians to utilize the system with minimal technical knowledge after a brief orientation.

Economic Feasibility

The proposed SolanaWatch system is considered economically feasible because it utilizes commercially available, low-cost IoT components. Based on the hardware bill of materials shown in Table 6, the hardware required to develop the prototype is approximately ₱7,208.00. Most of the software technologies used in the project, including Visual Studio Code, PlatformIO, React.js, Node.js, Turso libSQL, GitHub, and Vite, are available as free or open-source platforms, eliminating software licensing expenses. Although the initial investment is primarily attributed to the environmental sensors and solar power components, the system is expected to reduce crop losses caused by delayed disease detection, providing significant economic benefits to smallholder farmers through improved monitoring efficiency and timely preventive interventions.
